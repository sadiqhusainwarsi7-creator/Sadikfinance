Order Blocks Explained: Bullish, Bearish, and How to Trade Them

Order blocks are one of the most recognized — and most misused — concepts in ICT and SMC trading. This guide breaks down exactly what they are, how to separate a valid order block from a trap, and the entry models traders actually use once one forms.

13 min read / Updated June 2026 / By Sadik Finance Research

What Are Order Blocks?

An order block is the last candle, or small cluster of candles, in one direction immediately before price makes a sharp, decisive move in the opposite direction. The theory behind it is straightforward: that candle marks the approximate location where a large institutional order was placed, and because such orders are often too large to fill completely at one price, unfilled portions may still be resting in that same zone. When price eventually returns to it, those resting orders can cause a reaction again.

Think of an order block less as a magic zone and more as a footprint — a visible trace of where big money likely entered the market before pushing price away aggressively. The candle itself isn't special because of its shape; it's special because of what happened immediately after it.

Order blocks rarely work as a standalone signal. They're most reliable when they line up with other confirmations covered elsewhere in this library — a liquidity sweep, a break of structure, or a fair value gap sitting inside the same zone. We'll come back to that confluence requirement throughout this guide, because it's the difference between a high-probability order block and a coin flip.

Bullish Order Blocks

A bullish order block forms when price is moving down, prints one last bearish (down-close) candle, and then reverses sharply higher — often breaking a recent swing high in the process. That final down-close candle, right before the aggressive upward move, is marked as the bullish order block.

The logic: that candle is where sell-side liquidity was likely absorbed by large buy orders. Sellers pushed price down into a zone where institutional buyers were waiting, and once their orders were filled, the imbalance flipped and price snapped upward. When price later retraces back down into that same candle's range, it's viewed as a high-probability area for buyers to step back in.

Real Example

Suppose gold (XAU/USD) has been drifting lower across a few hourly candles. The final bearish candle closes, and the next candle gaps up and closes well above the previous candle's high, breaking the most recent swing high in the process. That last bearish candle is the bullish order block. If price pulls back into its range an hour later — ideally after sweeping a small pocket of sell-side liquidity on the way down — that retracement is the zone bullish traders watch for a long entry.

Bearish Order Blocks

A bearish order block is the mirror image: price is moving up, prints one last bullish (up-close) candle, and then reverses sharply lower, often breaking a recent swing low. That final up-close candle becomes the bearish order block — the zone where institutional sell orders likely absorbed the last wave of buying before price turned down hard.

When price retraces back up into that candle's range later, it's treated as a high-probability area for sellers to enter, particularly if that retracement also fills a fair value gap or follows a liquidity sweep of recent highs.

Real Example

On a Nasdaq (US100) 15-minute chart, price grinds higher for several candles, prints one strong bullish candle, then immediately reverses and breaks the prior swing low within the next two candles. That bullish candle is the bearish order block. A pullback into its upper half later in the session, especially one that also sweeps a small recent high first, is the classic short setup ICT and SMC traders look for.

Valid vs Invalid Order Blocks

Not every candle before a reversal qualifies as a usable order block. This is where most beginners go wrong — marking every wiggle on the chart as an order block until the chart is unreadable and the concept loses any predictive value. A handful of filters separate a genuinely valid order block from noise:

  • Displacement, not drift. The move away from the order block should be sharp and decisive — ideally breaking a nearby structural point — not a slow grind. A weak, gradual move afterward is a sign the zone has little institutional weight behind it.
  • Liquidity context. A valid order block usually forms right after a liquidity sweep — price grabbing a recent high or low just before reversing. An order block with no liquidity grab behind it is statistically weaker.
  • Imbalance behind it. The strongest order blocks often have a fair value gap sitting immediately after them, showing that the move away was aggressive enough to leave a visible imbalance.
  • Freshness. An order block that has already been tested and held once is weaker the second and third time price returns to it, since some of the resting orders behind it have likely already been filled.
  • Timeframe alignment. An order block on a 1-minute chart inside a strong daily trend in the opposite direction carries far less weight than one that aligns with the higher timeframe bias.

A useful mental filter: if you have to convince yourself a zone is an order block, it probably isn't a strong one. The clearest order blocks are visually obvious — a sharp rejection candle followed by an aggressive, structure-breaking move — not a subtle wiggle you have to squint at.

Entry Models for Trading Order Blocks

Once a valid order block is identified, traders generally use one of a few entry approaches rather than buying or selling the moment price touches the zone:

  1. Limit order at the zone. Placing a pending order at the edge of the order block (often the 50% level of its range) and letting price come to you, with a stop placed just beyond the block's far edge.
  2. Confirmation entry on a lower timeframe. Waiting for price to tap the order block and then print a lower timeframe structural shift or CISD candle sequence before entering, trading the confirmation rather than the raw zone.
  3. FVG-in-OB entry. When a fair value gap sits inside the order block's range, some traders wait specifically for price to fill that smaller gap rather than the entire order block range, tightening the entry and the stop loss.
  4. Liquidity sweep + OB combo. Waiting for price to first sweep a small intraday high or low and then react at the order block, treating the sweep as the final confirmation that smart money is active at that level.

Whichever model is used, the order block should define the trade's risk just as much as its entry — a stop loss placed just beyond the far side of the block keeps risk tied to the structure of the setup itself, rather than an arbitrary distance.

Managing a Trade After Entry

Getting the entry right is only half the job. Order block trades tend to work best when trade management mirrors the same logic used to find the entry in the first place:

  • Partial profit at the first opposing liquidity pool. If a bullish order block entry is taken, the first realistic target is often the nearest buy-side liquidity above — a recent swing high where opposing stop losses are likely resting.
  • Move the stop to break-even once structure confirms. After price breaks the next internal swing point in your favor, many traders tighten the stop to entry, removing downside risk while leaving the trade room to run.
  • Avoid moving the stop tighter than the order block's structure supports. A stop placed too close to current price, inside the noise of normal retracement, gets clipped by volatility that has nothing to do with the original thesis being wrong.
  • Reassess if a new order block forms against you. If price reverses and prints a fresh order block in the opposite direction before reaching your target, that's often a sign the original setup is losing momentum, even if your stop hasn't been hit yet.

None of this requires complex math — it requires treating the order block as the foundation of the entire trade idea, not just the trigger for clicking buy or sell.

Order Blocks Across Multiple Timeframes

A single order block can look completely different depending on which timeframe you're viewing it on. A 15-minute order block might sit comfortably inside a much larger 4-hour order block, and the two don't always agree. When they do align — a lower timeframe order block forming inside a higher timeframe one, in the same direction — the resulting confluence is generally treated as a stronger setup than either zone in isolation.

This is why many ICT and SMC traders work top-down: establish bias and locate the most significant order blocks on a 4-hour or daily chart first, then drop to a 5 or 15-minute chart purely to refine the entry once price reaches that higher timeframe zone, rather than hunting for order blocks on the lowest timeframe in isolation.

Key Takeaways

An order block is the last opposing candle before a sharp, structure-breaking move — a footprint of likely institutional entry, not a magic zone.

Bullish order blocks form on the last down-close candle before a strong move up; bearish order blocks form on the last up-close candle before a strong move down.

Validity depends on displacement, liquidity context, imbalance, freshness, and timeframe alignment — not every pre-reversal candle qualifies.

The strongest order blocks usually have a liquidity sweep behind them and a fair value gap sitting inside or right after them.

Entries should be planned with a specific model — limit order, confirmation entry, or sweep combo — rather than reacting the instant price touches the zone.

Common Mistakes With Order Blocks

1

Marking every candle before a bounce as an order block. This clutters the chart and destroys the concept's predictive value. Reserve the label for candles followed by genuine displacement and a structural break.

2

Ignoring liquidity context entirely. An order block that forms with no liquidity sweep behind it is statistically weaker than one that follows a clear stop-loss hunt. Skipping this check leads to lower quality setups.

3

Trading stale order blocks. Returning to the same zone for a third or fourth time, long after the original displacement, often means the resting orders behind it have already been filled and the zone has lost its edge.

4

Fighting the higher timeframe trend. A bullish order block on a 5-minute chart inside a strong daily downtrend is a much lower-probability trade than the same setup aligned with the dominant trend.

5

Using a stop loss unrelated to the block's structure. Placing a stop at an arbitrary pip or point distance instead of just beyond the order block's far edge disconnects risk management from the actual setup logic.

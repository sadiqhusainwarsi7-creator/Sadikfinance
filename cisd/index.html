CISD Explained: Reading the Exact Moment Delivery Flips

Change in State of Delivery (CISD) zooms into candle-by-candle behavior to time the precise moment buying pressure flips to selling pressure, or vice versa. This guide explains what it is, how it differs from MSS and BOS, and how to use it to tighten entries.

13 min read / Updated June 2026 / By Sadik Finance Research

What Is CISD?

Change in State of Delivery, almost always shortened to CISD, is a candle-level confirmation tool that identifies the exact moment aggressive buying intent flips to aggressive selling intent, or the reverse. Where most structural concepts in this library — market structure, BOS, MSS — operate on the scale of swing highs and swing lows, CISD zooms in much further, looking at a short sequence of individual candle opens and closes to pinpoint the precise candle where control changed hands.

The core mechanic is straightforward: during a short run of candles in one direction, CISD is confirmed the moment a candle closes back beyond the open of the most extreme candle in that prior run. That close is read as evidence that the side which was previously losing has now taken over — not just paused, but actually delivered price in the opposite direction with enough conviction to close through the most aggressive candle of the move that just ended.

CISD is best thought of as a magnifying glass placed on top of the bigger structural picture. It doesn't replace market structure, BOS, or MSS — it adds precision to them, answering the question "exactly which candle confirms this shift?" rather than "did a shift happen at some point in this general area?"

Why CISD Matters

CISD matters because it solves a real practical problem: structural breaks on a swing-point basis can be visually fuzzy when you're trying to time an actual entry. A break of structure might be confirmed by a candle close, but several candles often pass between the moment a reversal genuinely begins and the moment a swing high or low is officially broken. CISD gives traders a way to act earlier and with more precision, by reading the flip in delivery directly from the candles themselves rather than waiting for a more distant structural point to be taken out.

This added precision is particularly useful for entries, since a tighter, earlier confirmation point generally allows for a tighter stop loss and a better risk-to-reward ratio than waiting for a more conservative, later structural confirmation. The trade-off is that CISD, by working off less data than a full structural break, can occasionally fire on a flip that doesn't extend into a larger move — which is why it's typically used alongside the broader structural and liquidity context covered throughout this library, not in isolation.

Bullish CISD

A bullish CISD forms during a bearish run of candles. As price pushes down, a candle eventually closes back above the open of the most bearish candle in that down-move — typically the candle with the largest bearish body or the one that pushed furthest in the down direction. That close above the prior extreme's open is the bullish CISD confirmation, signaling that buying pressure has taken over from the selling pressure that was previously in control.

Real Example

Suppose GBP/USD prints four consecutive bearish candles on the 15-minute chart, with the third candle in that sequence being the largest, most aggressive bearish candle of the run. If the next candle closes back above the open of that third candle, that close is the bullish CISD confirmation — even though price hasn't yet broken the swing high that started the down-move. A trader using CISD would treat that close as the trigger to start watching for a long entry, well before a broader market structure shift might officially confirm on a swing-point basis.

Bearish CISD

A bearish CISD is the mirror case, forming during a bullish run of candles. As price pushes up, a candle eventually closes back below the open of the most bullish candle in that up-move — the candle that pushed furthest or had the largest bullish body. That close below the prior extreme's open is the bearish CISD confirmation, signaling that selling pressure has taken over.

Real Example

On a Nasdaq (US100) 5-minute chart, price prints three strong bullish candles in a row as it rallies into a known resistance area, with the second candle in that run being the largest. If the following candle closes back below the open of that second candle, that's the bearish CISD confirmation. A trader watching this would treat it as an early signal that sellers have reasserted control, potentially well before price breaks the most recent swing low on a larger timeframe.

CISD vs MSS

CISD and Market Structure Shift (MSS) both signal a potential reversal, but they operate at fundamentally different resolutions, and conflating them leads to confusion about which one should actually trigger an entry:

  • MSS: confirmed when price breaks a relevant internal swing point against the prevailing trend — a structural, swing-level event.
  • CISD: confirmed when a single candle closes back beyond the open of the most extreme candle in the immediately preceding run — a candle-level event that can occur before, after, or independent of any nearby swing point being broken.
  • Timing: CISD typically fires earlier than MSS, since it doesn't require an actual swing high or low to be taken out — only a shift visible in the candle sequence itself.
  • Reliability trade-off: MSS, requiring an actual structural break, is generally considered the more conservative and reliable signal; CISD trades some of that reliability for earlier timing and tighter potential stop placement.

Many traders use the two together rather than choosing one: CISD as the early trigger to start watching a setup closely, and MSS as the broader structural confirmation that the reversal has real follow-through behind it.

CISD vs BOS

The distinction against Break of Structure (BOS) follows a similar logic, but it's worth stating plainly because BOS and MSS are themselves easy to confuse with each other, let alone with CISD:

  • BOS: a structural break in the direction of the existing trend, confirming continuation — a swing-level event, just like MSS, but signaling the opposite implication (continuation rather than reversal).
  • CISD: a candle-level flip in delivery that can theoretically support either a continuation or a reversal read, depending on what the broader trend and structure context say about the direction it's flipping toward.
  • Practical relationship: a bullish CISD occurring inside an already-confirmed uptrend, during a pullback, often lines up with — and can even slightly precede — the next BOS to the upside, acting as an earlier entry trigger for what the BOS will later confirm on a structural basis.
  • What each answers: BOS answers "is the existing trend continuing?" CISD answers "exactly which candle shows delivery has flipped, regardless of which broader question that flip ends up supporting?"

CISD and Market Structure

CISD becomes considerably more reliable once it's read inside the context of the prevailing market structure rather than scanned for on every minor candle sequence. A bullish CISD that forms during a pullback inside an already-confirmed uptrend carries far more weight than the same candle pattern appearing with no clear higher timeframe trend behind it at all.

A practical sequence many traders follow: establish the higher timeframe trend and the most recent relevant swing points first, using the framework covered in our Market Structure guide, then drop to a lower timeframe specifically to look for a CISD confirmation that aligns with that established bias — rather than treating every candle-level flip on a low timeframe as equally significant regardless of the broader picture.

CISD Entry Models

CISD is most commonly used as a timing trigger layered on top of a setup that's already supported by other concepts in this library, rather than as a standalone reason to enter:

  1. Establish higher timeframe bias and a relevant point of interest first. An order block, a fair value gap, or a liquidity sweep zone gives CISD something meaningful to confirm.
  2. Wait for price to reach that zone. Don't look for CISD in the middle of a move with no nearby point of interest — the confirmation means far less without context.
  3. Identify the CISD confirmation candle. Once price is at the zone, watch the candle sequence for a close back beyond the open of the most extreme opposing candle.
  4. Enter on or shortly after the CISD close. Some traders enter at the close of the confirmation candle itself; others wait for a small retracement into the body of that candle for a slightly improved entry price.
  5. Place the stop beyond the extreme of the run CISD just reversed. The high or low of the candle sequence that CISD confirmed against gives a structurally meaningful invalidation point.

Real Trading Examples

A fuller walk-through shows how CISD typically gets layered with other concepts in a live setting rather than traded in isolation.

Real Example

Consider gold (XAU/USD) inside a confirmed 4-hour uptrend. Price pulls back into a bullish order block on the 15-minute chart that also overlaps with a fair value gap from the original rally. As price taps that zone, it prints two small bearish candles, then a slightly larger bearish candle, before a strong bullish candle closes back above the open of that larger bearish candle — a bullish CISD confirmation, right inside the order block and FVG overlap. A trader following this sequence would treat the CISD close as the entry trigger, with a stop placed just below the low of the bearish candle run that CISD just reversed, rather than waiting for a full swing-level MSS confirmation that might only arrive several candles later, after a meaningfully worse entry price.

Key Takeaways

CISD identifies the exact candle where delivery flips, confirmed when a candle closes back beyond the open of the most extreme candle in the prior run.

CISD operates at the candle level, while MSS and BOS operate at the swing-structure level — CISD typically fires earlier but trades some reliability for that earlier timing.

MSS signals a potential reversal against the trend; BOS signals continuation with the trend; CISD can support either reading depending on the broader structural context.

CISD is most reliable when it occurs at an existing point of interest — an order block, FVG, or liquidity sweep zone — rather than scanned for on every candle sequence.

Many traders use CISD as an early entry trigger and MSS as the broader structural confirmation, rather than choosing one tool over the other.

Common CISD Mistakes

1

Looking for CISD on every minor candle sequence. Scanning for a delivery flip on every small run of candles, with no higher timeframe context or nearby point of interest, produces a large number of low-quality signals that don't reflect genuine institutional behavior.

2

Confusing CISD with a full structural shift. Treating every CISD confirmation as equivalent to a confirmed MSS overstates what a single candle close actually proves, since CISD can occasionally flip without the broader structure ever following through.

3

Ignoring the broader trend before acting on CISD. A bearish CISD appearing during a strong, confirmed uptrend pullback carries far less weight than the same pattern appearing with the trend, and trading it without that context increases the odds of fighting the dominant order flow.

4

Using a stop loss unrelated to the CISD candle sequence. Placing a stop at an arbitrary distance instead of beyond the high or low of the run CISD just reversed disconnects the trade's risk from the actual structural logic of the setup.

5

Treating CISD as a standalone setup with no point of interest. Acting on a delivery flip that occurs in the middle of a move, with no order block, FVG, or liquidity sweep nearby, removes most of the context that makes CISD a meaningful confirmation rather than just noise.

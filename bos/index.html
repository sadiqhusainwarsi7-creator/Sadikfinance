What Is Break of Structure (BOS)?

Break of Structure, almost always shortened to BOS, is the objective, on-chart confirmation that the current trend is continuing. It happens when price closes beyond the most recent relevant swing point in the same direction the market was already moving. If the market is in an uptrend printing higher highs and higher lows, a BOS is the formation of a fresh higher high. If the market is in a downtrend printing lower highs and lower lows, a BOS is the formation of a fresh lower low.

BOS sits right at the center of the ICT and SMC structural framework. Before you can trade liquidity sweeps, order blocks, or Fair Value Gaps with any real confidence, you need a reliable way to confirm that the broader directional bias is still intact — and BOS is exactly that confirmation tool.

The foundational rule: BOS always agrees with the existing trend. If a break goes against the trend instead, that is not a BOS — it is a Market Structure Shift (MSS), covered later in this guide.

Why BOS Matters

It is tempting to treat BOS as a minor technicality — just a label for "price made a new high." In practice, it does three things that matter enormously to how you build a trading process around structure:

  • It confirms directional bias objectively. Instead of guessing whether a trend is "still good," BOS gives you a binary, rules-based answer: did price close beyond the prior swing point or not.
  • It anchors your entry zones. The impulsive move that creates a BOS almost always leaves behind an order block or Fair Value Gap — the exact area you will look to enter on the next pullback.
  • It separates real continuation from a failed attempt. A break that fails to close beyond the level, and instead reverses, is a strong clue that liquidity was simply being swept rather than a genuine trend extension taking place.

Every other concept in this curriculum — liquidity, order blocks, FVGs, CISD — is interpreted relative to whether structure has actually broken. Get comfortable with BOS first, and everything downstream becomes far easier to apply with confidence.

Bullish BOS

A bullish BOS forms during an established uptrend. The sequence looks like this: price prints a higher high (HH), pulls back to form a higher low (HL), and then pushes upward again. The moment a candle closes above that prior HH — not just wicks above it — the bullish BOS is confirmed.

Traders use a confirmed bullish BOS as structural permission to start looking for long entries on the next retracement, ideally into the bullish order block or Fair Value Gap created by the very candle (or candles) that produced the break.

Bullish BOS Checklist

Confirming a Genuine Bullish Break

Existing HH/HL sequence in place · Pullback holds the most recent HL · Strong-bodied candle closes above the prior HH · Impulse leaves behind an FVG or order block to use as an entry zone

Bearish BOS

A bearish BOS is the mirror image, occurring during an established downtrend. Price prints a lower low (LL), retraces to a lower high (LH), and then pushes downward again. The candle close beneath the prior LL — again, a close, not merely a wick — confirms the bearish BOS.

Just as with the bullish version, short-side traders treat a confirmed bearish BOS as the green light to look for entries on the next retracement upward, ideally into the bearish order block or FVG left behind by the breakdown candle.

Bearish BOS Checklist

Confirming a Genuine Bearish Break

Existing LL/LH sequence in place · Retracement holds the most recent LH · Strong-bodied candle closes below the prior LL · Impulse leaves behind an FVG or order block to use as an entry zone

BOS vs. Liquidity Sweep

This is the single most common point of confusion for traders learning structure for the first time. A liquidity sweep and a BOS can look almost identical in the moment they happen — both involve price trading through a prior high or low — but they carry very different implications, and the difference comes down to confirmation and follow-through.

Characteristic BOS Liquidity Sweep
Candle behaviourCloses beyond the level with a strong bodyOften a sharp wick through the level that reverses
Follow-throughContinuation in the same directionFrequently reverses shortly after
What it signalsTrend continuation confirmedTrapped liquidity, often before a reversal
Typical contextInside an already-confirmed trendAt an obvious, heavily tested high/low

Critical warning: Many failed BOS attempts are simply liquidity sweeps in disguise. Price pushes through a swing point, fails to close beyond it with conviction, and reverses instead. Waiting for an actual candle close — not a wick — is the simplest filter for telling the two apart. See our Liquidity Masterclass for the full sweep framework.

BOS vs. MSS

BOS and MSS use the exact same underlying mechanic — a candle closing beyond a swing point — but they point in opposite directions and carry opposite implications. Understanding this distinction clearly will save you from constantly second-guessing whether a break you're looking at is good news or a warning sign for your current bias.

Characteristic BOS MSS
Direction vs. trendSame direction (continuation)Opposite direction (potential reversal)
What it confirmsTrend remains intactControl may be transferring to the other side
Trader responseLook for entries in the trend directionWait for further confirmation before acting
Risk if misreadLower — aligns with dominant flowHigher — can be a temporary pullback, not a real reversal

In short: if the break agrees with the trend you are already in, it's a BOS. If it goes against that trend, it's an MSS — and it demands a much stricter confirmation process before you risk any capital on it.

BOS and Market Structure

BOS cannot be understood in isolation from the broader concept of market structure — the ongoing sequence of higher highs, higher lows, lower highs, and lower lows that defines trend direction at any given moment. BOS is simply the event that extends that sequence one swing point further in the direction it was already heading.

This is why context always matters more than the break itself. A BOS that occurs on a 15-minute chart but contradicts the dominant 4-hour or daily trend is statistically far less reliable than a 15-minute BOS that confirms and aligns with that higher-timeframe direction. Before trusting any BOS signal, always ask: does this agree with what structure looks like one or two timeframes higher?

BOS Entry Models

Knowing what a BOS is matters far less than knowing what to do once you see one. The following three entry models cover the vast majority of how structure-based traders actually use BOS in live trading.

Model 1 — The Order Block Retest

After a confirmed BOS, mark the last opposing candle (or small cluster of candles) immediately before the impulsive breakout move — this is the order block. Wait for price to retrace back into that zone before entering in the direction of the break, with a stop placed just beyond the far side of the order block.

Model 2 — The FVG Retracement

The same impulsive move that creates a BOS frequently leaves behind a Fair Value Gap. Many traders prefer entering on a retracement into this gap — either a full fill or a retrace to the gap's midpoint — rather than the order block itself, since the FVG often offers a tighter, more precise entry zone. See the FVG Guide for the complete mechanics.

Model 3 — The Break-and-Retest

The simplest model: wait for the BOS candle to close beyond the swing point, then wait for price to come back and "retest" that exact broken level from the other side before continuing. This model sacrifices some entry precision for simplicity and is often the easiest starting point for traders new to structure-based entries.

Risk Management Note

Always Define Risk Before the Entry Confirms

Regardless of which model you use, your stop should sit beyond the most recent opposing swing point or the far edge of your entry zone — never an arbitrary number of pips or points disconnected from the structure that justified the trade.

Real Trading Examples

Example 1 — Bullish BOS on EUR/USD (1H)

Price builds a clean HH/HL sequence across the London session. A shallow pullback holds the most recent HL, and a strong bullish candle closes above the prior HH, confirming the BOS. The candle's impulsive move leaves behind a Fair Value Gap directly beneath the new high. A trader following Model 2 waits for price to retrace into that FVG, enters long with a stop beneath the most recent HL, and targets the next untaken liquidity pool above.

Example 2 — Bearish BOS on Gold Futures (15M)

Price is already in a downtrend, printing a clean LL/LH sequence. A sharp bearish candle closes beneath the most recent LL, confirming the bearish BOS. The order block formed by the candle immediately preceding that move becomes the entry zone. A trader following Model 1 shorts the retest of that order block, with a stop above the most recent LH and a target at the next sell-side liquidity pool below.

Common BOS Mistakes

  • Confirming BOS on a wick instead of a close: A wick that pokes beyond a swing point without an actual candle close is not yet a valid BOS — it could easily turn into a liquidity sweep instead.
  • Chasing the breakout candle itself: Entering directly on the BOS candle, rather than waiting for a retracement into an FVG or order block, often means entering at the worst possible price with the least favorable risk-to-reward.
  • Ignoring the higher-timeframe trend: A lower-timeframe BOS that contradicts the dominant higher-timeframe direction is statistically far less reliable than one that aligns with it.
  • Mislabeling a sweep as a BOS: Treating a quick, wick-driven sweep of a swing point as trend confirmation — rather than waiting for a genuine candle close — leads to entries right before a reversal.
  • Trading every minor swing break as a BOS: Not every tiny internal swing break is structurally significant. Reserve real conviction for breaks of swing points that matter on your trading timeframe.

Key Takeaways
  • BOS confirms trend continuation — a new HH in an uptrend, or a new LL in a downtrend.
  • A genuine BOS requires a candle close beyond the swing point, not just a wick.
  • A failed BOS attempt is often the same thing as a liquidity sweep — context and follow-through tell them apart.
  • BOS confirms continuation; MSS signals a potential reversal — same mechanic, opposite meaning.
  • Enter on the retracement into the resulting FVG or order block, not directly on the breakout candle.
  • Always align lower-timeframe BOS signals with the higher-timeframe trend before trusting them.

Funded Account Guide: How Challenges, Drawdown Rules, and Payouts Actually Work

A funded account lets you trade a prop firm's capital instead of your own, in exchange for following a specific rule set. This guide walks through how evaluations actually work, the rules that decide pass or fail, and how payouts and scaling plans function once you're funded.

14 min read / Updated June 2026 / By Sadik Finance Research

What Is a Funded Account?

A funded account is a trading account where the capital belongs to a proprietary trading firm rather than the trader. In exchange for access to that capital — often ranging from $10,000 to $200,000 or more — the trader agrees to follow the firm's specific risk rules and typically keeps a majority share of the profits generated, commonly somewhere between 70% and 90%, depending on the firm and plan.

The appeal is straightforward: a trader with a genuine edge but limited personal capital can trade a much larger account size than they could otherwise afford, while the firm's exposure is limited by the strict drawdown rules covered later in this guide. It's a structure that works because both sides have aligned incentives — the firm profits from a share of genuinely skilled traders' results, and the trader gains access to capital they couldn't otherwise risk.

How Prop Firms Work

Most retail-facing prop firms operate on a simulated funding model rather than directly handing traders live institutional capital from day one. A trader pays a one-time fee to attempt an evaluation, trades a demo or simulated account against a specific rule set, and if they pass, the firm grants access to a funded account that mirrors real market conditions, with real payouts paid out from the firm's own capital based on the trader's simulated performance.

This model lets firms onboard a large pool of traders cheaply, filter for the ones who can demonstrate consistent, rule-following performance during the evaluation, and only commit firm capital — or real profit-sharing payouts — to the traders who actually clear that bar. It's worth understanding this structure because it explains why the evaluation rules are designed the way they are: every rule exists specifically to filter out behavior the firm doesn't want to fund.

Evaluation Challenges Explained

An evaluation challenge is the test a trader completes before being granted a funded account. Challenges vary by firm but generally follow one of two structures:

  • Two-step challenges. A first phase with a profit target (often 8–10%) and a second, slightly easier phase with a lower target (often 4–5%), both under the same drawdown rules. This remains the more traditional model.
  • One-step challenges. A single phase combining a profit target with the full set of drawdown and consistency rules, completed in one pass rather than two. These have become increasingly common as firms compete on speed-to-funding.

Regardless of structure, the underlying goal is the same: demonstrate that profit can be generated while staying inside a defined risk envelope, consistently enough that the performance looks like skill rather than a single lucky run.

Profit Targets

A profit target is the percentage gain a trader must reach during an evaluation phase to pass. Common figures are 8–10% for a challenge's first phase and 4–5% for a second phase on two-step models, or a single 8–10% target on one-step models. These targets are deliberately calibrated to be reachable by a trader with a genuine edge and sound risk management, while remaining difficult enough to filter out undisciplined or purely lucky attempts.

It's worth resisting the temptation to treat the profit target as something to rush toward as quickly as possible. A trader chasing an 8% target in the first few days, taking oversized risk to get there faster, is far more likely to breach a drawdown rule before reaching the target than a trader who paces the same target across several weeks using consistent, modest position sizing.

Maximum Drawdown Rules

Maximum drawdown is the total amount an account is allowed to lose from either its starting balance or its highest point reached (called a "trailing" drawdown) before the evaluation — or the funded account itself — is automatically failed. Common figures range from 8% to 12% of the initial account size, though the exact rule and calculation method vary meaningfully by firm.

The distinction between a static drawdown (measured only from the starting balance) and a trailing drawdown (measured from the account's peak equity) matters significantly. On a trailing drawdown rule, growing an account to +5% and then losing 10% from that peak can breach the limit even though the account is still technically profitable overall relative to the starting balance — a detail that catches many traders who assume drawdown only applies below the original deposit.

Daily Drawdown Rules

Daily drawdown caps the loss allowed within a single trading day, separate from the overall maximum drawdown limit. Common figures sit around 4–5% of account size. Some firms calculate this from the previous day's closing balance, while others track it intraday in real time — meaning even a temporary dip during the day, not just the day's final result, can breach the rule on some firms' systems.

Real Example

On a $50,000 account with a 5% daily drawdown limit ($2,500), a trader who is down $1,800 mid-session and then recovers to close the day down only $400 might still have breached the rule on a firm that tracks intraday drawdown in real time, even though the day's final result looks mild. Understanding exactly how a specific firm calculates this rule — end-of-day balance versus real-time tracking — is essential before risking a full day's worth of trades against it.

Consistency Rules

Consistency rules cap how much of an account's total profit can come from a single trading day, specifically to prevent one large, high-risk trade from carrying an entire evaluation or payout. A common structure limits any single day's profit to 20–30% of the total profit accumulated across the challenge, meaning a trader needs genuinely consistent performance across multiple days rather than one outsized win.

This rule directly targets a specific kind of evaluation gaming: taking a single oversized, high-risk trade hoping to hit the entire profit target in one shot. Firms that enforce consistency rules will fail or flag an account that technically reached its profit target this way, even if the maximum drawdown limit was never breached, because the result doesn't reflect the kind of repeatable behavior the firm is actually trying to fund.

Common Reasons Traders Fail Challenges

The vast majority of failed evaluations trace back to a small set of recurring causes, most of which connect directly to concepts covered elsewhere in this library:

  • Oversized risk per trade in an attempt to reach the profit target quickly, breaching maximum or daily drawdown after just a few losing trades.
  • Revenge trading after an early loss, covered in detail in our Trading Psychology guide, compounding risk at exactly the wrong moment.
  • Ignoring the specific drawdown calculation method a firm uses — assuming a static drawdown rule when the firm actually uses a trailing one, for example.
  • Violating consistency rules by reaching the profit target through one or two outsized trades rather than steady, repeatable performance.
  • Trading news events or holding through high-impact releases without checking whether the firm restricts this, since some firms prohibit or penalize trading around major scheduled news.

Risk Management for Funded Accounts

The most reliable approach to a funded evaluation is applying the same disciplined risk management covered in our dedicated Risk Management guide — generally keeping per-trade risk around 0.5–1% of account size — rather than taking larger risks simply because the capital isn't personally owned. This isn't just a moral stance; it's the mathematically sound approach, since the firm's drawdown rules are specifically designed to fail accounts taking outsized risk, whether those accounts are winning or losing in the moment.

A useful mental reframe: treat the account size on a funded challenge exactly as you would treat the equivalent amount of personal capital, applying the same 1% rule, the same daily loss limits, and the same stop-loss discipline. Traders who maintain this consistency between personal and funded account behavior tend to have a meaningfully higher pass rate than those who deliberately take larger risks specifically because the money isn't their own.

Passing a Challenge Step-by-Step

A practical, methodical approach to a challenge generally follows this sequence:

  1. Read the specific firm's full rule set before trading a single position. Confirm the exact profit target, maximum drawdown type (static or trailing), daily drawdown calculation method, and any consistency or news-trading restrictions.
  2. Set a fixed per-trade risk percentage, typically 0.5–1%, and calculate position size for every trade using that fixed percentage rather than estimating.
  3. Set a personal daily loss limit slightly tighter than the firm's rule, giving a buffer against the firm's hard limit rather than trading right up to the edge of it.
  4. Pace the profit target across multiple weeks rather than rushing it, taking only setups that meet the same quality criteria used in personal trading.
  5. Track every trade in a journal, including the emotional state behind each entry, watching specifically for the revenge-trading and overtrading patterns covered in our Trading Psychology guide.
  6. Reduce size, not increase it, as the profit target gets closer, since protecting progress already made matters more at that stage than reaching the target a few days sooner.

Payouts and Scaling Plans

Once funded, a trader typically becomes eligible for payouts on a recurring schedule — commonly every two weeks or monthly, depending on the firm — withdrawing their share of the profits generated, often 70–90% depending on the specific plan. Some firms also offer an accelerated first payout as an incentive, while standard payout cycles apply from that point forward.

Many firms also offer scaling plans, where the funded account size increases — sometimes doubling — after a trader hits a specific profit milestone over a set number of payout cycles, typically while maintaining the same risk discipline that passed the original evaluation. Scaling plans reward exactly the kind of consistent, controlled performance this guide has emphasized throughout: a trader who reaches the milestone through steady gains with controlled drawdown is in a far stronger position to scale than one who reaches the same milestone through a few high-risk trades that happened to work out. Common Mistakes

1

Rushing the profit target with oversized risk. Taking larger-than-normal positions specifically to reach the target faster is the single most common cause of breaching a drawdown rule before the evaluation is even close to complete.

2

Misunderstanding which drawdown calculation method applies. Assuming a static drawdown rule when the firm actually uses a trailing calculation from the account's peak equity has caught many otherwise profitable traders off guard.

3

Ignoring consistency rules while chasing the profit target. Hitting the target through one or two outsized trades, rather than steady performance across multiple days, can fail an evaluation under a firm's consistency rule even without breaching drawdown.

4

Treating funded capital with less discipline than personal capital. Taking larger risks specifically because the money belongs to the firm ignores that the entire rule set is designed to identify and fail exactly this behavior.

5

Not reading the specific firm's full rule set before trading. Rules around news trading, weekend holding, and drawdown calculation vary meaningfully between firms, and assuming one firm's rules apply universally to all of them is a common and avoidable mistake.

Key Takeaways

A funded account gives traders access to a prop firm's capital after passing an evaluation, in exchange for following a strict drawdown and risk rule set.

Profit targets, maximum drawdown, daily drawdown, and consistency rules each exist to filter for repeatable skill rather than a single lucky or oversized result.

The most common reason challenges fail is oversized risk taken to reach the profit target quickly, which is also what breaches drawdown rules the fastest.

Applying the same disciplined risk management used on a personal account — rather than taking larger risks because the capital isn't personally owned — is the more mathematically sound approach to passing.

Once funded, payouts and scaling plans reward consistent, controlled performance over time rather than a single strong result, mirroring the same discipline required to pass the original evaluation.

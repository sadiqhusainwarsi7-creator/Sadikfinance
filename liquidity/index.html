What Is Liquidity in Trading?

In financial markets, liquidity refers to the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold without significantly affecting its price. But in the ICT framework, liquidity means something far more specific and far more tradeable: it refers to the pools of resting orders — predominantly stop-loss orders — that accumulate above swing highs and below swing lows across all timeframes.

These pools exist because of predictable retail trader behaviour. When most traders place a long position, they put their stop-loss below a recent low. When they go short, they place a stop above a recent high. Multiply this across thousands of retail traders looking at the same charts and the same "obvious" levels, and you get massive clusters of stop orders sitting at predictable locations in the market.

Institutions — banks, hedge funds, and algorithmic desks — need these stop orders. They cannot fill a 10,000-lot position at a single price without moving the market against themselves. The solution? Engineer a move toward a liquidity pool, trigger the retail stop orders, fill their institutional position against the flood of orders being released, and then reverse price toward their true target.

The foundational truth: Every significant price move in the market is, at its core, a move toward liquidity. Understanding this single principle will change how you read every chart you ever look at.

This is why ICT traders stop chasing breakouts and start asking a different question: Where are the stop losses resting? And where does price need to go to take them? Once you can answer that question with confidence, you are trading with institutional intent rather than against it.

The Three Types of Liquidity You Must Know

ICT breaks liquidity down into three core categories, each with distinct characteristics and trading implications. Mastering all three is non-negotiable for applying the framework at a high level.

1. Buy-Side Liquidity (BSL)

Buy-side liquidity rests above the market — specifically above swing highs, equal highs, prior day highs, prior week highs, and any obvious resistance level where retail traders have placed their stop losses on short positions. Buy orders from retail traders who are entering breakouts also cluster here.

When you see a clean double top or equal highs on a chart, that is not just a resistance level — it is a liquidity magnet. Every short seller who entered at that level has their stop-loss sitting just above those highs. Institutions targeting BSL will push price upward through that level, trigger all those stops, fill their sell orders against the rush of buy orders being released, and then deliver price downward.

BSL Identification

Where Buy-Side Liquidity Forms

Equal highs (two or more touches at the same price level) · Prior day/week/month highs · Swing highs with multiple rejection wicks · Round number psychological levels above price · Trendline break entry stops

The key visual cue for a BSL target is equal highs — when price has tested the same high two or more times without breaking through. Every failed attempt trains retail traders to place stops just above. By the time price reaches that level on the third or fourth attempt, the stop cluster above is enormous. That is exactly why institutions target it.

2. Sell-Side Liquidity (SSL)

Sell-side liquidity rests below the market — below swing lows, equal lows, prior day lows, obvious support levels, and any area where retail long traders have clustered their stop-losses. It is the mirror image of BSL, and it operates on the same institutional logic.

When you see a clean double bottom or equal lows on a chart, retail traders have repeatedly entered long from that level, placing their stops just below it. That creates a pool of sell orders waiting to be triggered. Institutions targeting SSL push price below those lows, trigger the stop cascade, fill their buy orders against the flood of sell orders, and reverse price upward.

SSL Identification

Where Sell-Side Liquidity Forms

Equal lows (two or more tests at the same level) · Prior day/week/month lows · Swing lows with long lower wicks · Round number psychological levels below price · Support level stop clusters

3. Draw on Liquidity (DOL)

The Draw on Liquidity is the most powerful concept in the liquidity framework. It is not a level — it is a narrative. The DOL is the next significant pool of liquidity that price is being delivered toward on a higher timeframe. It is your answer to the question: "Where is price going, and why?"

Every trade you take should have a clearly identified DOL. Your entry might be on a 5-minute chart, but the DOL is identified on the daily or 4-hour. The DOL tells you the target — the pool of liquidity that price must reach to complete the current delivery cycle. Without identifying the DOL, you have an entry but no real target, and no high-probability way to manage the trade.

DOL in practice: If the daily chart shows BSL (equal highs) above current price and price has just swept a SSL pool below, the Draw on Liquidity is the BSL above. Every lower timeframe setup should be taken in alignment with this draw — long entries only, targeting the equal highs above.

How Liquidity Pools Form and Why

Understanding why liquidity pools form — not just where — gives you a massive edge in anticipating institutional moves before they happen.

Retail Trading Patterns Create Predictable Clusters

Retail traders, on the whole, use the same charting platforms, the same popular indicators, and the same basic concepts. Support and resistance, double tops and bottoms, trendlines — these concepts are taught in virtually every retail trading course. The result is that millions of traders are looking at the same levels and placing their stops at the same locations.

This isn't a conspiracy — it's a structural reality. When 50,000 traders all see the same "obvious" support level and go long from it, placing their stops 10 pips below, the resulting stop cluster is enormous. And it's entirely predictable to anyone with a chart and the understanding that this is how retail traders behave.

The Liquidity Sweep — Reading the Hunt

The liquidity sweep is the moment of execution — when price finally reaches a liquidity pool, triggers the resting orders, and then reverses. Learning to read sweeps in real time is one of the most valuable skills in the ICT framework.

Anatomy of a Perfect Liquidity Sweep

A high-quality liquidity sweep has five distinct characteristics that separate it from a genuine breakout:

  • Price aggressively takes out the pool level — a sharp, fast move through the equal highs or lows, not a slow grind
  • The wick is disproportionate — the candle that sweeps the level often has an exaggerated wick relative to the body
  • Immediate reversal — price does not consolidate above/below the swept level. It closes back through it within the same or the next one to two candles
  • Volume spike — if you have access to volume data, a genuine sweep is accompanied by a significant volume spike as retail stops trigger
  • Displacement follows — after the reversal, price moves aggressively in the opposite direction, creating FVGs and leaving behind the MSS that confirms the sweep is complete

Critical warning: Not every break of a high or low is a liquidity sweep. Genuine trending moves also break through prior highs and lows — those are BOS (Break of Structure) events that confirm the trend. The key difference is what happens immediately after: does price close back through the broken level (sweep) or continue beyond it (BOS)?

How to Trade Liquidity — Entry Framework

Knowing where liquidity is and how sweeps work is useless without a precise entry framework. Here is how ICT traders translate liquidity knowledge into actual trades.

Step 1 — Identify the HTF Draw on Liquidity

Before touching a lower timeframe, identify the DOL on the daily chart. Is there BSL above (equal highs, prior week high) or SSL below (equal lows, prior week low) that price is more likely to be delivered toward? This is your directional bias. Only take trades that align with this draw.

Step 2 — Wait for the Counter-Move Sweep

With your DOL identified, wait for price to first sweep the opposite side of liquidity before moving toward your target. If the DOL is BSL above, price will often first sweep SSL below — clearing out the weak long hands before the real move up. This counter-sweep is your entry trigger zone.

Step 3 — Confirm with an MSS

After the sweep, drop to the 5-minute or 1-minute chart and wait for a Market Structure Shift (MSS). The MSS is the first lower timeframe signal that institutional buying or selling has begun in earnest. Do not enter before the MSS — you're trying to catch the reversal too early without confirmation.

Step 4 — Enter at the Displacement FVG or OB

The displacement that creates the MSS leaves behind a Fair Value Gap (FVG) or Order Block (OB). Enter on the retracement into this zone. Your stop goes below the sweep low (for longs) or above the sweep high (for shorts). Your target is the DOL identified in Step 1.

Real Trade Examples — BSL and SSL in Action

Example 1 — Forex: London SSL Sweep into NY Bullish Delivery

Setup: EURUSD daily chart shows equal lows (SSL) from the previous week sitting below current price. The prior month high (BSL) is sitting well above. HTF bias is bullish — DOL is the prior month high.

London open arrives. Price pushes down sharply, breaks below the equal lows (SSL sweep), wicks through and closes back above. On the 5-minute chart, a bullish MSS prints as price displaces upward, leaving behind a bullish FVG. Entry is taken on the retracement into the 5M FVG. Stop is placed below the sweep low. Target is the prior month high (BSL) — a clean 1:4 risk-reward setup.

Example 2 — Futures: NQ Equal Highs BSL Raid

Setup: NQ (Nasdaq futures) has formed equal highs on the 1-hour chart over two days. The equal highs represent a massive BSL pool. The daily chart is bearish — DOL is the equal lows below. Bearish bias confirmed.

During the New York AM kill zone, price pushes above the equal highs (BSL sweep), wicks through, and closes back below within the same 5-minute candle. A bearish MSS prints on the 1-minute chart. Short entry at the 1M OB formed during the displacement. Stop above the sweep high. Target is the equal lows below — another 1:3+ setup.

Advanced DOL — Stacking Multiple Targets

As you advance in your understanding of liquidity, you learn to stack multiple DOL targets. Price rarely moves from one liquidity pool to the next in a straight line — it often sweeps one pool, retraces, then continues to the next.

Common Liquidity Mistakes

  • Selling the breakout: One of the most expensive mistakes in trading. When price breaks above resistance (equal highs), retail traders sell expecting a reversal. But if that's the DOL and price has swept SSL below first, the breakout is real. The BSL sweep scenario only applies when the DOL is below.
  • Entering before confirmation: The sweep alone is not an entry signal. You must wait for the MSS on a lower timeframe to confirm the reversal. Entering on the wick of the sweep candle is gambling — the sweep could extend further before reversing.
  • Ignoring the DOL: Taking a trade because you see a sweep without first identifying the DOL. If the DOL is in the same direction as the sweep, you're trying to fade institutional intent. That is the losing side of the trade.
  • Trading every equal high/low: Not every set of equal highs or lows will be swept. Price often moves through them directly as a BOS. The key filter is the HTF context — is the DOL above (making equal highs a target) or below (making them a potential sweep before continuing down)?
  • Placing stops too tight: When trading a sweep, your stop must go beyond the swept level — below the wick low for longs, above the wick high for shorts. Placing stops inside the sweep range is almost guaranteed to result in stop-outs before the trade moves in your direction.

Key Takeaways
  • Liquidity is resting stop-loss orders — not abstract supply and demand. It is real, predictable, and tradeable.
  • BSL lives above equal highs and prior swing highs. SSL lives below equal lows and prior swing lows.
  • The Draw on Liquidity (DOL) is the next significant pool price is being delivered toward — identify it on the daily first.
  • A liquidity sweep = price aggressively takes out a pool, then closes back through it immediately. Genuine BOS = price closes beyond the level and continues.
  • Never enter on a sweep alone — wait for the MSS confirmation on a lower timeframe before triggering.
  • Your stop always goes beyond the swept level. Your target is the next DOL pool, identified on the higher timeframe.
  • When multiple liquidity pools align at the same price (equal high + prior week high + daily FVG), that is the highest-value target on the chart.

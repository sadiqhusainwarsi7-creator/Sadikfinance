Smart Money Concepts distils the best of institutional analysis into a structured, learnable system. This guide takes you from zero to a working SMC framework — no fluff, no indicators.

What Is Smart Money Concepts?

Smart Money Concepts, commonly abbreviated as SMC, is a retail trading framework that models the behaviour of institutional market participants — the banks, hedge funds, central banks, and proprietary trading firms whose collective order flow genuinely moves price. The term "smart money" is not a compliment so much as a descriptor: these are entities with access to superior information, enormous capital, and the infrastructure to execute trades at scale in ways that ordinary retail participants cannot match.

SMC grew organically out of the ICT methodology developed by Michael J. Huddleston but was refined and popularised within the broader online trading community as a more streamlined, accessible system. Where ICT is a vast and evolving curriculum encompassing dozens of models, time theories, and seasonal tendencies, SMC distils the most repeatable and learnable elements into a coherent framework: understand market structure, map liquidity, identify where institutions left their footprint, wait for confirmation, then enter with a clearly defined risk.

The reason SMC has resonated so strongly with traders across skill levels is that it addresses a problem traditional technical analysis never properly solved: why does a setup fail even when everything looks perfect? Standard TA gives you patterns and probabilities. SMC gives you a causal model. When a "support level" breaks and immediately reverses — trapping every trader who shorted the breakdown — SMC explains it precisely: that was a sell-side liquidity sweep, engineered to fill institutional buy orders before the real move higher began. Once you see markets through this lens, patterns you once dismissed as noise become legible, predictable events.

This guide is designed to take you from zero to a working understanding of the complete SMC framework — structure, liquidity, entry models, risk management, and confluence stacking. If you have already read the ICT Concepts guide, this will feel like a natural progression. If you are coming to SMC first, you will find everything you need here, with references to the underlying ICT concepts where appropriate.

The core idea: institutions cannot hide their footprint. Every engineered move, every stop hunt, every price imbalance leaves a traceable mark on the chart. SMC teaches you to read those marks systematically and trade with the institutions, not against them.

Structure First — The Bedrock of Every SMC Decision

The non-negotiable foundation of any SMC trade is a clearly defined directional bias derived from market structure. This sounds simple, but the majority of losing traders skip or rush this step. They see a bullish order block on a 15-minute chart and jump straight into a long trade — not realising that the daily and 4H charts are in a clearly bearish structure, and the 15M setup is nothing more than a counter-trend bounce that will be overwhelmed.

SMC uses the same structural vocabulary as ICT: swing highs and swing lows, breaks of structure (BOS), market structure shifts (MSS), and changes in state of delivery (CISD). But it applies these with a rigid top-down hierarchy. Start on the weekly chart to identify the macro trend — is price making higher highs and higher lows, or lower highs and lower lows? Drop to the daily chart to understand the intermediate narrative within that macro trend. The 4H chart shows you the specific price swing you are currently in and reveals which structural level will be targeted next. The 1H chart is where the setup begins to form. The 15M or 5M is where you execute.

Every timeframe must tell a consistent story for a trade to qualify as high-conviction. If the weekly is bullish, the daily is pulling back into a demand zone, the 4H has just printed a bullish BOS, the 1H is forming a bullish order block, and the 15M gives a CISD confirmation — that is five timeframes aligned in your favour. These are the setups worth waiting weeks for, because they produce the cleanest entries with the tightest stops and the largest potential targets.

SMC Core Rule

The Top-Down Hierarchy

Weekly/Daily → define macro bias and major liquidity targets. 4H/1H → identify the specific structural swing and point of interest (POI) to trade. 15M/5M → wait for a lower-timeframe confirmation (CISD, MSS, or engulfing candle) before entering. Each timeframe validates the one below it. Skip this process and you are gambling, not trading.

A concrete example: in early 2024, GBP/USD was in a bullish weekly and daily structure. The 4H pulled back into a clear demand zone created by a prior BOS. The 1H formed a bullish order block within that zone. Traders who identified this setup on the 15M — watching for a CISD to confirm the reversal — entered long with a stop below the order block, targeting the sell-side liquidity above the recent equal highs. The trade resolved in three to four sessions, delivering a risk-reward ratio of approximately 1:4. Every element was visible in advance because the structure was read correctly from the top down.

Liquidity Pools — Mapping the Market's Hidden Targets

If structure is the framework, liquidity is the fuel. Understanding liquidity is what elevates an SMC trader from someone who can identify setups to someone who can anticipate where price will move before it gets there. This is the concept that, once internalised, makes the market feel fundamentally different to navigate.

Institutions — the banks and funds trading billions in notional value — cannot fill positions the way retail traders do. A fund manager who wants to acquire $1 billion in EUR/USD long exposure cannot hit "buy market" and be done with it. The market impact of that trade would be catastrophic to their average entry price. Instead, they need a vast pool of sellers on the other side to absorb their buy orders. Those sellers are retail traders whose stop-losses are triggered when price dips below a key level. The institution engineers that dip — sweeping the stops, absorbing the sell orders — and simultaneously accumulates their long position at a price well below where price will trade once the sweep is complete.

This is the mechanism behind every stop hunt you have ever experienced. It was not bad luck. It was not your indicator failing. It was a deliberate, engineered event to serve institutional order flow. SMC teaches you to map these events before they happen — and to position yourself on the right side of them.

  • Buy-side liquidity (BSL) clusters above swing highs, double tops, equal highs, and psychologically significant round numbers. Every retail short in the market has a stop above these levels. When price spikes above them — often as a sharp wick — it has swept BSL to fill institutional sell orders before a move lower. The "false breakout to new highs" that burned you was smart money distributing into your buy stop.
  • Sell-side liquidity (SSL) clusters below swing lows, double bottoms, equal lows, and round numbers ending in 00 or 000. Retail longs accumulate stops here. A wick below these levels followed by an immediate bullish reclaim is SSL being swept to fill institutional buy orders before a move higher. This is the mechanism behind every frustrating "stopped out by one pip" experience.
  • Liquidity raid — the deliberate wick that breaches a pool, triggers the clustered stops, and immediately reverses. The speed of the reversal is key: the faster and more decisive the reclaim, the more convinced you can be that the sweep was institutional in nature.
  • Inducement is a more advanced concept and one of the most practically valuable. Before sweeping the primary, large liquidity pool, institutions often engineer a smaller sweep — a minor equal high or low slightly in front of the main target — to draw in impatient traders who see the first sweep and rush to enter. These early entrants are then stopped out as price continues to the actual target. Identifying inducement prevents you from being the early entry that gets used as fuel for the real move.

To practise mapping liquidity, open any major pair on a 4H chart and mark every pair of equal highs and equal lows you can see going back 60 to 90 days. Then study what price did when it reached those levels. In the vast majority of cases on highly liquid instruments, price will wick through them — sometimes briefly, sometimes by 5–10 pips — before reversing. Once you see this pattern consistently, your entire relationship with "support and resistance" will change permanently.

SMC Entry Models — The Four Primary Setups

With structure mapped and liquidity pools identified, the next discipline is learning to wait for a specific, objective trigger before placing a trade. SMC uses several entry models, each representing a moment where institutional activity is confirmed on a lower timeframe after the higher-timeframe narrative has aligned. Patience here is everything — the quality of your entry determines the quality of your risk-reward ratio.

01

Order Block (OB) Mitigation

The OB entry is the cornerstone of SMC trading. After identifying the last opposing candle before an impulsive move — the order block — you wait for price to retrace into that zone. The trigger is a lower-timeframe confirmation within the OB, typically a bullish or bearish CISD on the 5M or 15M chart. For example: daily structure is bullish, price has pulled back to a 4H bullish order block in the discount zone. On the 15M, price enters the OB and prints a bullish candle that closes through the body of the prior bearish candle (CISD). That is your signal to enter long, with a stop just below the bottom of the order block.

02

Fair Value Gap (FVG) Fill

The FVG entry exploits price inefficiencies. After identifying a three-candle imbalance on your analysis timeframe, you wait for price to return and fill the gap. The entry trigger is a rejection within the gap — either a lower-timeframe CISD, a long-wicked candle rejecting the 50% or 100% level of the gap, or a full candle close inside the gap followed by a directional candle. FVGs nested inside order blocks are the highest-quality version of this setup: price is simultaneously mitigating an OB and filling an FVG, providing double confirmation of institutional interest at that exact price level.

03

Liquidity Sweep + Reversal

This is perhaps the most powerful and satisfying entry model in SMC because it aligns your entry with the exact moment smart money is filling their position. After identifying a key liquidity pool (equal lows, swing low, round number support), you wait for price to wick below it — sweeping the SSL — then form a bullish MSS or CISD on the 5M or 15M as it reclaims the swept level. The stop goes just below the wick low. The target is the next BSL pool above. Because you are entering at the exact price where institutions accumulated, you often get immediate follow-through with minimal drawdown. A classic version: GBP/USD holds a tight range for two sessions with four equal lows at 1.2710. Price wicks to 1.2698, sweeps all four stop clusters, then immediately prints a bullish engulfing candle on the 15M reclaiming 1.2710. Enter long, stop at 1.2694, target the equal highs at 1.2780.

04

Break of Structure (BOS) Retest

After a confirmed BOS — where price closes beyond a significant prior swing high or low — it frequently retraces to retest the broken level before continuing. This retest level often aligns with an order block or FVG left during the original break, making it a zone of multiple confluence. The BOS retest entry requires patience: you wait for price to return to the broken level after the BOS candle closes, confirm that the level is holding via a lower-timeframe structural shift, and enter in the direction of the break. This model is ideal in trending markets where you have missed the initial move and need a re-entry point.

Risk Management — The Part That Makes Everything Else Work

A perfectly identified setup with poor risk management is still a losing proposition over time. SMC's great gift is that it provides natural, logical locations for every component of a trade — not just the entry, but the stop-loss, the targets, and the position size. This removes the guesswork that plagues most retail traders and replaces it with objective, repeatable rules.

Stop-loss placement in SMC is always structural. For an OB entry, the stop goes below the low of the bullish order block (for longs) or above the high of the bearish order block (for shorts). For a liquidity sweep entry, the stop goes below the wick that swept the SSL (for longs) or above the wick that swept the BSL (for shorts). For a BOS retest, the stop goes below the prior swing low that must hold for the BOS to remain valid. These locations are not arbitrary; they are the exact points at which your trade thesis is invalidated. If price reaches your stop, the structural reason for the trade no longer exists.

Position sizing rule: never risk more than 1% of your account on a single trade. This is not conservative — it is mathematically necessary. Even with a 65% win rate, a drawdown of ten to fifteen consecutive losses is statistically possible. At 1% risk per trade, a fifteen-trade losing streak reduces your account by 14% — painful but recoverable. At 5% risk, the same streak reduces your account by 54%, from which most traders psychologically and financially never recover.

Profit targets in SMC are always the next liquidity pool in the direction of the trade. For a long trade, the target is the nearest BSL cluster — the equal highs, swing high, or round-number resistance above price where retail shorts will have clustered their stops. For a short trade, the target is the nearest SSL cluster below. This keeps targets objective and grounded in market reality rather than arbitrary reward ratios. In practice, most quality SMC setups offer risk-reward ratios of 1:3 to 1:8 when the entry is precise and the target is the correct liquidity pool — meaning you can be profitable over the long term even winning fewer than half your trades.

Trade management is the final piece. Once a trade has moved in your favour by one to one and a half times your initial risk, move your stop to break-even. This converts the trade from a risk position to a free trade. If the instrument has multiple liquidity pools between your entry and your final target, consider taking partial profits at each one — securing gains while leaving a runner position to capture the full move. This approach smooths equity curve volatility and prevents the psychologically corrosive experience of watching a large winner turn into a loser.

Building a Confluence Checklist — How the Best SMC Traders Think

The most consistent SMC traders share one characteristic: they are extraordinarily selective. They do not trade every setup that looks acceptable. They wait, sometimes for days, for the specific intersection of conditions that tips the probability heavily in their favour. This selectivity is not inactivity — it is professional discipline. A sniper does not fire at every movement; they wait for the moment when all conditions align for a clean, high-confidence shot.

Building your personal confluence checklist is one of the most valuable exercises you can undertake as an SMC student. Start with the non-negotiables — the conditions that must always be present — and add refinements that you have personally validated through backtesting and journalling. Here is a framework to begin from:

  • Higher-timeframe bias confirmed: weekly and daily structure are clearly bullish or bearish, and the trade direction aligns with that bias. Counter-trend setups require at least an MSS on the daily to justify consideration.
  • Liquidity has been swept or inducement has cleared: the pool that would have stopped you out has already been hit, removing that risk from the trade. The best setups often form immediately after a sweep.
  • Price location — discount for buys, premium for sells: use the 50% midpoint of the relevant range to filter entries. This alone eliminates a huge percentage of low-quality setups that look attractive in isolation.
  • A defined point of interest (POI) at the entry zone: an order block, a fair value gap, a breaker block, or a combination of these. The POI is the structural reason price should react at this level.
  • Lower-timeframe confirmation: a CISD, MSS, or high-conviction engulfing candle on the 5M or 15M that confirms the reversal has begun. Never enter a POI on limit alone — wait for price to show you that it is actually reacting.
  • Session alignment: the trade is active during London (7 AM–12 PM GMT) or New York (1 PM–5 PM GMT) open — the two windows when institutional order flow is at its peak and SMC setups resolve most cleanly.
  • Clean risk-reward: the stop is tight (because the entry is precise), and the target liquidity pool is at least three times the stop distance away. If you cannot achieve 1:3 with logical stops and targets, the setup does not meet the minimum threshold.

When four or more of these conditions align simultaneously, the setup graduates from speculative to high-conviction. Keep a detailed trade log — not just wins and losses, but which confluences were present for every trade, how the setup resolved, and what you could improve. Over 50 to 100 trades, patterns will emerge: you will discover which specific combination of conditions produces your best results in your chosen market, and you will build a ruleset that is uniquely calibrated to your trading style and schedule. That personalised ruleset, derived from your own data, is worth more than any generic SMC strategy you could ever find online.

Key Takeaways
  • SMC is a distilled version of ICT — a systematic, repeatable framework for trading with institutional order flow.
  • Top-down analysis is mandatory — higher timeframe bias always takes priority over lower timeframe signals.
  • Liquidity pools (buy-side and sell-side) are the targets smart money hunts — learn to map them before they are hit.
  • Entry models (OB, FVG, sweep + reversal, BOS retest) provide objective, repeatable setups with defined risk.
  • Risk management: stops at structural logic, targets at the next liquidity pool, 1% max risk per trade.
  • Confluence stacking — structure + liquidity + OB/FVG + session timing — is the hallmark of elite SMC trading.

Frequently Asked Questions

SMC is not identical to ICT, but it is built on the same conceptual foundation and uses much of the same vocabulary. ICT — the Inner Circle Trader curriculum developed by Michael Huddleston — is a vast, evolving body of work that includes dozens of specific models, time-cycle theories (Quarterly Theory, Power of 3), seasonal tendencies, and an entire framework for understanding central bank manipulation. SMC is a community-refined distillation: it takes the most repeatable and learnable elements of ICT — market structure, liquidity mapping, order blocks, fair value gaps, and premium/discount arrays — and packages them into a cleaner, more accessible system. If you have read this guide and the ICT Concepts guide on this site, you are not learning two different things. You are learning the same underlying market logic from two slightly different angles, and the overlap will deepen your understanding of both.
With a structured approach and genuine commitment, most dedicated students see their analysis quality improve significantly within two to three months, and reach consistent demo profitability within four to six months. The word "consistent" is important here — it means not just winning trades, but applying the framework correctly, managing risk precisely, and making the same quality decisions regardless of the previous trade's outcome. Moving from consistent demo performance to consistent live profitability typically takes another three to six months, as psychological pressures of real money introduce a new set of challenges. The most common reason students take longer is over-trading and concept-jumping — trying too many setups before mastering the fundamentals. If you commit to one entry model (the liquidity sweep reversal is a good starting point), one market, and one session window for the first three months, your progress will accelerate dramatically.
SMC works wherever institutional participation is high and consistent. The top-tier choices for most SMC traders are: EUR/USD and GBP/USD (deepest Forex liquidity, extremely clean structure), NAS100 (excellent volatility during New York open, very clear liquidity engineering), XAUUSD / Gold (famous for stop hunts and FVG fills, highly responsive to SMC concepts), and SPX500 (strong institutional narrative, works well for swing trading). Cryptocurrency markets like BTC/USD are viable but carry more noise and a higher prevalence of artificial manipulation outside traditional ICT killzones. The single most important factor is choosing a market you can monitor during its primary institutional session — there is no point trading the London session on a market that is primarily driven by Tokyo flow. Pick one market, trade it at its best time, and build your edge there before diversifying.

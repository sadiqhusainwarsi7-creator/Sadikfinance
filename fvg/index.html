Fair Value Gap (FVG) Explained: Price's Unfinished Business

A Fair Value Gap is the visible imbalance left behind when price moves so fast that two-sided trading never really happened at certain prices. This guide explains how FVGs form, why price tends to return to them, and how they combine with structure, liquidity, displacement, and order blocks.

13 min read / Updated June 2026 / By Sadik Finance Research

What Is a Fair Value Gap (FVG)?

A Fair Value Gap, almost always shortened to FVG, is a three-candle price pattern where the wick of the first candle doesn't overlap with the wick of the third candle, leaving a visible gap between them. That gap represents a price range where genuine two-sided trading — buyers and sellers actually negotiating a price together — barely happened, because the move through that zone was too fast and too one-directional for the usual back-and-forth to occur.

The "fair value" in the name refers to the idea that a price range with no real two-sided negotiation hasn't been properly "agreed upon" by the market yet. ICT theory holds that markets tend to be inefficient when an imbalance like this exists, and that price often returns to at least partially fill that gap later — not because of some mystical pull, but because unfilled orders and the simple tendency of price to revisit unfinished business make a partial retracement into the gap a common occurrence.

Mechanically, the gap is measured between the high of the first candle and the low of the third candle in a bullish FVG, or between the low of the first candle and the high of the third candle in a bearish FVG. The middle candle is almost always the large, aggressive one responsible for creating the imbalance in the first place.

Why FVGs Form

FVGs form because of imbalance between buying and selling pressure during a fast move. In normal, healthy price action, candles overlap with each other — buyers push up, sellers push back, and a genuine price gets negotiated at most levels along the way. When one side overwhelms the other so completely that price simply skips through a range without that negotiation happening, a gap is left behind.

This is why FVGs are so closely tied to the concept of displacement covered elsewhere in this library — a fair value gap is, in effect, physical evidence that displacement occurred at that exact point on the chart. Large, aggressive moves driven by genuine institutional conviction tend to leave large, clean gaps. Slow, hesitant moves tend to leave small gaps or none at all.

Bullish FVG

A bullish FVG forms during an aggressive upward move, where the high of the first candle in the three-candle sequence sits below the low of the third candle, leaving an unfilled gap between them. This gap is viewed as a potential support zone — an area buyers may step back in if price retraces down into it, since the imbalance there hasn't been "fairly" traded yet.

Real Example

Suppose EUR/USD prints three candles in quick succession during a strong rally: the first candle has a high of 1.0850, the second is a large bullish candle that pushes well beyond that level, and the third candle has a low of 1.0875. Because 1.0850 to 1.0875 was never traded through with any real two-sided activity, that 25-pip range is the bullish FVG. If price later pulls back into that zone, traders watching this setup would view it as a potential area for buyers to re-enter, particularly if it overlaps with other bullish confluence like a recent liquidity sweep.

Bearish FVG

A bearish FVG is the mirror case, forming during an aggressive downward move where the low of the first candle sits above the high of the third candle. This gap is viewed as a potential resistance zone — an area sellers may step back in if price retraces up into it.

Real Example

On a Nasdaq (US100) 15-minute chart, price drops sharply across three candles: the first candle has a low of 18,420, the second is a large bearish candle that pushes well below that level, and the third candle has a high of 18,395. The 18,395 to 18,420 range was never properly traded through, making it a bearish FVG. A retracement back up into that zone would be watched by sellers as a potential area to re-enter short, especially if it lines up with a swept high or a bearish order block in the same range.

FVG and Market Structure

Not every fair value gap carries equal weight, and market structure is one of the main filters that separates a meaningful FVG from a minor one. An FVG that forms during a move that also breaks a relevant swing point — confirming a structural shift or continuation — is generally treated as far more significant than a gap that forms in the middle of a range with no structural break attached to it at all.

A practical approach: before treating any FVG as a serious point of interest, check whether the move that created it also produced a meaningful structural break. If it did, the gap is more likely to represent genuine institutional footprint rather than minor noise. The complete framework for reading these structural breaks is covered in the dedicated Market Structure guide.

FVG and Liquidity

FVGs become considerably stronger points of interest when they form immediately after a liquidity sweep, rather than appearing with no liquidity context behind them. A bullish FVG that forms right after price sweeps a recent low — taking out sell-side liquidity before reversing — combines two separate pieces of evidence: a stop-hunt confirming smart money activity, and an imbalance confirming the reversal away from that sweep was genuinely aggressive.

This combination, sweep followed immediately by an FVG in the opposite direction, is one of the more commonly cited high-probability patterns across ICT and SMC education, precisely because it stacks two independent confirmations rather than relying on either one alone. The full mechanics of how liquidity pools form and get swept are covered in the Liquidity Masterclass.

FVG and Displacement

The relationship between FVGs and displacement is about as direct as any two concepts get in this library: an FVG cannot exist without displacement, because the gap is, by definition, the imbalance that an aggressive, fast move leaves behind. Strong displacement — large candle bodies, minimal overlap, real conviction — tends to leave large, clean fair value gaps. Weak, hesitant price action leaves small gaps or none at all.

This relationship is genuinely useful as a two-way confirmation check. If you're unsure whether a move counts as real displacement, check whether it left behind a visible FVG — if there's no gap at all, the move likely lacked the conviction needed to qualify. Conversely, when grading the quality of an FVG itself, a larger gap generally implies stronger displacement behind it than a thin, barely-visible one. The complete criteria for what separates strong from weak displacement are covered in the dedicated Displacement guide.

FVG Entry Models

Trading an FVG generally follows one of a few approaches, rather than buying or selling the instant price touches any part of the gap:

  1. 50% of the gap (consequent encroachment). Many traders treat the midpoint of the FVG, rather than its full range, as the more precise area to watch for a reaction, since price often only needs to fill half the imbalance before continuing in the original direction.
  2. Confirmation entry on a lower timeframe. Waiting for price to tap the FVG and then print a CISD candle sequence or small lower timeframe structural shift before entering, rather than reacting to the raw gap alone.
  3. FVG-in-order-block entry. When a fair value gap sits inside a larger order block's range, some traders wait specifically for price to reach that smaller, more precise overlap rather than the entire order block.
  4. Liquidity sweep plus FVG combo. Treating a fair value gap that formed immediately after a liquidity sweep as a higher-priority entry zone than an FVG with no nearby sweep behind it.

Whichever model is used, a stop loss placed just beyond the far edge of the gap — or the structural point the move that created it was confirming — keeps risk tied to the actual logic of the setup rather than an arbitrary distance.

FVG + Order Block Confluence

One of the most frequently cited high-probability combinations in ICT and SMC trading is a fair value gap that sits inside, or directly overlaps with, a valid order block. This overlap combines two independent pieces of evidence at the same price: the order block suggests where a large institutional order likely entered the market, and the FVG confirms that the move away from that entry was aggressive enough to leave a genuine imbalance behind.

When this confluence is present, many traders treat the smaller, overlapping zone — rather than the entire order block range — as the higher-precision area to watch for an entry, since it represents the part of the order block most directly tied to genuine displacement. An order block with no FVG anywhere near it is not automatically invalid, but the added confluence of an overlapping gap is generally considered a meaningful upgrade in setup quality.

Real Trading Examples

A fuller walk-through helps show how FVGs typically combine with other concepts in a live setting rather than being traded as an isolated pattern.

Real Example

Consider gold (XAU/USD) on the 1-hour chart inside a broader daily uptrend. Price sweeps a recent intraday low, triggering stop losses from earlier longs. The next three candles show strong bullish displacement, breaking the most recent swing high and leaving a clean bullish FVG behind, with the last bearish candle before that displacement also qualifying as a bullish order block. The FVG sits directly inside that order block's range. A trader following this sequence would mark the overlapping FVG-and-order-block zone as the primary point of interest, wait for price to retrace into it — ideally to the 50% level of the FVG — and look for a CISD confirmation at that exact zone before entering long, using the sweep, the displacement, the FVG, and the order block together as layered confirmation rather than any single concept in isolation.

Key Takeaways

A Fair Value Gap is the visible imbalance left between three candles when price moves too fast for genuine two-sided trading to occur at certain prices.

FVGs cannot exist without displacement — the size and cleanliness of a gap is a direct reflection of how aggressive the move that created it actually was.

An FVG carries more weight when it forms after a liquidity sweep, aligns with a structural break, or overlaps with a valid order block, rather than appearing in isolation.

The 50% level of an FVG (consequent encroachment) is often treated as the more precise area to watch than the full gap range.

FVG and order block overlap is one of the most cited high-probability confluences in ICT and SMC trading, combining institutional footprint with confirmed displacement at the same price.

Common FVG Mistakes

1

Marking every small candle gap as a tradable FVG. Not every minor three-candle imbalance reflects genuine institutional displacement. Filtering for size, context, and confluence avoids cluttering the chart with low-quality zones.

2

Ignoring whether the FVG aligns with the higher timeframe trend. A bullish FVG forming during a clear daily downtrend carries far less weight than the same gap forming during a confirmed uptrend, even if the three-candle pattern looks identical.

3

Expecting price to fill the entire gap every time. Price often only needs to retrace into the 50% level of an FVG before continuing in the original direction. Waiting for a full fill can mean missing the actual entry opportunity.

4

Trading an FVG with no liquidity or structural context. An isolated gap with no nearby sweep, structural break, or order block overlap is a lower-quality signal than one that stacks several of these confirmations together.

5

Forgetting that FVGs can be invalidated. If price trades cleanly through an entire FVG without reacting, the zone has effectively been consumed and shouldn't be treated as a fresh point of interest on a later retest.

IFVG Explained: What Happens When a Fair Value Gap Fails

An Inverse Fair Value Gap forms when a fair value gap fails to hold and price trades straight through it, flipping the zone's role entirely. This guide explains how that failure happens, how IFVG differs from a standard FVG, and how to build entries around it.

13 min read / Updated June 2026 / By Sadik Finance Research

What Is an Inverse Fair Value Gap (IFVG)?

An Inverse Fair Value Gap, almost always shortened to IFVG, is a standard fair value gap that has failed to act as support or resistance and instead been traded straight through by price. Once that failure is confirmed, the same gap flips roles entirely: a bullish FVG that fails becomes a bearish IFVG, and a bearish FVG that fails becomes a bullish IFVG.

The logic mirrors a relationship covered elsewhere in this library between order blocks and breaker blocks. Just as a breaker forms when an order block fails and the orders defending it get trapped, an IFVG forms when a fair value gap fails and the traders who expected it to hold get caught on the wrong side of the market. That trapped positioning becomes a new source of pressure, and the gap — now an IFVG — is watched for a reaction in the opposite direction on a later retest.

How an FVG Becomes an IFVG

The transition from FVG to IFVG happens in a specific sequence, and skipping steps in that sequence is a common source of false signals. A genuine IFVG requires more than a brief wick through the gap — it requires price to trade through the entire gap and follow through with a confirmed structural break in the same direction.

  1. A standard FVG forms during a displacement move, as covered in the FVG Guide, and is expected to act as support (bullish FVG) or resistance (bearish FVG) on a later retest.
  2. Price returns to the gap but, instead of reacting the way the original gap would suggest, trades straight through its full range.
  3. A relevant swing point breaks as part of that same move, confirming a genuine structural shift rather than a deeper-than-usual retracement that might still reverse back in the original direction.
  4. The failed gap is relabeled as an IFVG, with its role flipped to match the new direction confirmed by the structural break.

Without that structural confirmation, a wick through an FVG is simply a deeper test of the gap, not evidence that it has genuinely failed. This distinction matters because treating every minor overshoot as a confirmed IFVG produces far more false signals than the concept actually generates when applied with this filter in place.

Bullish IFVG

A bullish IFVG forms when a bearish FVG — originally expected to act as resistance — fails to hold. Instead of reversing lower as the gap would suggest, price trades straight through it, breaks a recent swing low, and confirms a bullish structural shift. The zone that just failed to act as resistance is now treated as a bullish IFVG, watched for support if price pulls back into it.

Real Example

Suppose GBP/USD rallies into a bearish FVG that formed during an earlier sell-off, right where sellers would typically be expected to step back in. Instead of reversing, price trades cleanly through the entire gap, sweeps a nearby swing low on the way, and then breaks the most recent internal swing high — confirming a bullish shift. That former bearish FVG is now a bullish IFVG. A pullback into it afterward is treated as a potential long entry, not an area to expect resistance.

Bearish IFVG

A bearish IFVG is the mirror case: a bullish FVG — originally expected to act as support — fails to hold. Price trades straight through it, breaks a recent swing high to the downside, and confirms a bearish structural shift. That former bullish FVG is now treated as resistance, watched for a reaction lower on a retest.

Real Example

On a Bitcoin (BTC/USD) 4-hour chart, price pulls back into a bullish FVG that has held on one prior test. This time, price trades straight through the gap without reacting, then breaks the most recent swing low, confirming a bearish shift. That gap has now become a bearish IFVG. If price retraces back up into it later, sellers watch it as a fresh area of resistance, rather than assuming the old "support" label still applies just because it held once before.

IFVG vs FVG

The distinction is straightforward once stated plainly, but it's a common point of confusion for traders newer to the concept:

  • FVG: a zone that has not yet failed — it represents an untested or successfully defended imbalance, expected to hold on a retest.
  • IFVG: a zone that has already failed — price traded through it, trapping the positioning that expected it to hold, and the zone has flipped roles.
  • Direction: an FVG keeps its original bias until it fails. An IFVG, by definition, has the opposite bias from what the original FVG had.
  • Confirmation required: an IFVG only counts once a structural break confirms the failure — a wick through the gap with no follow-through doesn't create a valid IFVG.
  • Typical reliability: some traders treat a confirmed IFVG as carrying more weight than an untested FVG, since it comes with direct evidence that one side of the market has already failed to defend that exact price range.

A simple way to keep the two separate: an FVG is a prediction about where an imbalance might cause a reaction. An IFVG is evidence — it already showed you which side lost control inside that exact range.

IFVG and Market Structure

Market structure is what ultimately confirms whether an IFVG is genuine. As covered above, a wick through a fair value gap with no structural follow-through is not enough on its own — the defining requirement is that price closes beyond the gap and that move also breaks a relevant swing point, confirming a real shift rather than just a deeper retracement.

This is why IFVG, like its order block counterpart the breaker block, depends heavily on correctly reading structure first. A practical approach: identify the FVG and watch how price behaves when it returns to that zone, using the framework in our Market Structure guide to judge whether any break that follows genuinely qualifies as structural or is simply noise.

IFVG and Liquidity

IFVGs are generally considered more reliable when the failure of the original FVG coincides with a liquidity sweep, rather than occurring in isolation. A bearish FVG failing right as price also sweeps a nearby swing low, for example, combines two pieces of evidence: a stop-hunt confirming smart money activity, and the gap's failure confirming that the reversal away from that sweep was genuine rather than a brief overshoot.

This is the same layered logic that runs throughout this library — a single concept in isolation is weaker evidence than several concepts converging at the same price and the same time. The full mechanics of how liquidity pools form and get swept are covered in the Liquidity Masterclass.

IFVG Entry Models

Trading an IFVG generally follows a clear sequence rather than reacting the instant the original gap is broken:

  1. Identify the original FVG and track how price behaves when it returns to that zone.
  2. Confirm the failure. Wait for price to trade through the entire gap and break a relevant swing point — not just wick through part of the range.
  3. Mark the IFVG using the same boundaries as the original gap, now with the flipped bias.
  4. Wait for the retest rather than chasing the move that just confirmed the failure.
  5. Look for added confirmation at the retest — a CISD candle sequence or a small liquidity sweep just before the zone strengthens the entry.
  6. Place the stop beyond the IFVG's far edge, keeping risk tied to the structure of the zone itself rather than an arbitrary distance.

IFVG + Order Block Confluence

Just as a standard FVG carries more weight when it overlaps with a valid order block, an IFVG carries more weight when it overlaps with a breaker block — the order block equivalent of a failed zone. When a fair value gap and an order block fail at the same price and in the same structural move, the resulting IFVG and breaker often occupy the same general zone, and that combined failure is treated as a stronger signal than either one failing in isolation.

This overlap effectively stacks two independent confirmations of the same underlying event: the breaker shows that an institutional entry zone failed, and the IFVG shows that the imbalance accompanying that entry also failed. Traders who track both concepts together, rather than treating order flow and imbalance as separate categories, tend to catch this confluence more consistently than those checking for only one or the other.

Real Example

Consider the S&P 500 (US500) on the 1-hour chart. Price has been ranging, with a bullish FVG sitting from an earlier rally. Price drifts back down into that gap, and rather than holding as support, trades straight through it, sweeps a nearby swing low in the process, and breaks the most recent internal swing low to the downside, confirming a bearish structural shift. That former bullish FVG is now a bearish IFVG. The last bullish candle before the move down also qualifies as a bearish order block, overlapping with the IFVG almost exactly. A trader following this sequence would mark the combined IFVG-and-breaker-style zone as the primary point of interest, wait for a retracement back up into it, and look for a CISD confirmation there before entering short — using the liquidity sweep, the structural break, the IFVG, and the order block together as layered evidence rather than any single signal alone.

Key Takeaways

An IFVG is a fair value gap that has failed to hold and flipped roles, much like a breaker block is a failed order block.

Bullish IFVGs come from failed bearish FVGs; bearish IFVGs come from failed bullish FVGs.

A valid IFVG requires price to trade through the entire gap and confirm a relevant structural break — a brief wick through part of the range isn't enough.

IFVGs carry more weight when the failure coincides with a liquidity sweep or overlaps with a breaker block at the same price.

Execution should wait for a retest of the IFVG with added confirmation, rather than entering the moment the original gap is broken.

Common IFVG Mistakes

1

Calling every wick through an FVG a confirmed IFVG. A brief overshoot that doesn't break a relevant swing point isn't a genuine failure — it may simply be a deeper-than-usual retracement that still respects the original gap's intended direction.

2

Chasing the break instead of waiting for the retest. Entering the moment price trades through the original FVG, rather than waiting for the retest of the new IFVG zone, often means a worse entry price and a wider, less structurally meaningful stop.

3

Forgetting that the bias flips. Continuing to treat a former bullish FVG as support after it has clearly failed and flipped into a bearish IFVG means trading directly against the new order flow.

4

Ignoring the higher timeframe trend. A bullish IFVG forming inside a dominant daily downtrend carries far less weight than one aligned with the broader trend, even if the lower timeframe structure looks clean.

5

Treating IFVG and breaker block confluence as guaranteed. Assuming every failed FVG will overlap neatly with a failed order block overstates how often this specific confluence actually occurs — it's a bonus when present, not a requirement for an IFVG to be valid.

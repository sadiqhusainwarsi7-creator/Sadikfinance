Premium & Discount Explained: Buying Cheap and Selling Expensive, Defined

Premium and Discount split any trading range into two halves around its midpoint, giving you a simple filter for whether a price is statistically expensive or cheap relative to the range it's in. This guide explains equilibrium, both zones, and exactly how institutions use them.

12 min read / Updated June 2026 / By Sadik Finance Research

What Is Premium & Discount?

Premium and Discount is a simple but powerful idea: take any defined price range — usually the distance between a significant swing high and swing low — and split it exactly in half. The upper half is the premium zone, where price is statistically expensive relative to that range. The lower half is the discount zone, where price is statistically cheap relative to that same range.

The concept doesn't tell you whether price will go up or down on its own. What it tells you is where within a range a buy or sell makes more statistical sense. Buying in premium, near the top of a range, means you're paying close to the most expensive price available in that range. Selling in discount, near the bottom, means you're giving away the position close to the cheapest price available. Premium and Discount is the filter that helps you avoid doing exactly that.

This concept pairs naturally with almost everything else in this library. Liquidity, order blocks, and fair value gaps tell you where a reaction might happen. Premium and Discount tells you whether that reaction is happening at a statistically sensible price within the broader range, or whether you'd be entering at a poor relative price even if every other signal lines up.

Equilibrium: The 50% Level

Equilibrium is the exact midpoint of a defined range — the line that separates premium from discount. It's calculated simply: take the high of the range, take the low of the range, and find the price exactly halfway between them. On most charting platforms, this is the same calculation a standard Fibonacci retracement tool uses for its 50% level, which is why many ICT and SMC traders use a Fibonacci tool purely to locate equilibrium rather than for any of its other ratios.

Equilibrium itself is often treated as a decision point rather than a trade zone. Price crossing from discount into premium, or vice versa, can be an early sign that sentiment within that range is shifting. Some traders also watch for price to react directly at equilibrium as a sign that neither buyers nor sellers have a clear statistical edge at that exact level — making it a less attractive entry zone than a clear premium or discount extreme.

Premium Zone

The premium zone is the upper half of a defined range — anywhere above the 50% equilibrium line, up to the range's high. Price trading in premium is, by definition, in the more expensive half of that range. This doesn't mean price can't go higher; ranges expand and old highs get broken constantly. It means that, relative to the current range, a long entry taken deep in premium is statistically a worse price than the same long entry would have been in discount.

Premium is generally treated as the more attractive area to look for selling opportunities, particularly when it lines up with other bearish confluence such as a liquidity sweep above a prior high, a bearish order block, or a market structure shift to the downside. Selling in premium means giving away a position close to the most expensive price the range has offered.

Discount Zone

The discount zone is the lower half of a defined range — anywhere below the 50% equilibrium line, down to the range's low. Price trading in discount is in the statistically cheaper half of that range. As with premium, this doesn't guarantee a bounce; price can and does continue lower past old lows. It simply means a long entry taken in discount is, relative to the defined range, a better price than the same entry taken in premium.

Discount is generally treated as the more attractive area to look for buying opportunities, especially when combined with other bullish confluence such as a swept low, a bullish order block, or a confirmed structural shift back to the upside. Buying in discount means entering close to the cheapest price the range has offered.

Real Example

Suppose GBP/USD rallies from 1.2400 to 1.2600 over a session, then begins pulling back. The equilibrium of that 200-pip range sits at 1.2500. If price pulls back to 1.2530, it's still in premium — a statistically less attractive area to buy, even if the pullback looks tempting. If price instead pulls all the way back to 1.2440, it's now in discount, and a bullish order block or FVG sitting in that zone carries more weight than the same setup would have closer to the range high.

Why Institutions Buy in Discount

Large institutional buyers think in terms of average entry price across a large position, not a single fill. Buying deep in a range, in discount, gives them room to scale into a position at progressively better prices if the market continues lower, and a more favorable average price if the market reverses from there. Buying in premium, near the top of a recent range, leaves far less room for error — any reversal immediately puts a large position underwater.

This is also where liquidity context reinforces Premium and Discount directly: institutions often wait for price to sweep a discount-zone low — taking out the stop losses of traders who sold that low expecting further downside — before stepping in to buy. The combination of a liquidity sweep happening specifically inside the discount zone is considered a stronger signal than either concept appearing on its own.

Why Institutions Sell in Premium

The same logic applies in reverse for selling. Distributing a large short position deep in premium, near the top of a range, gives an institution room to add to the position at progressively better average prices if price continues higher, and a strong average entry if price reverses from there. Selling in discount, near the bottom of a range, leaves very little room before the position is already at risk.

As with discount-zone buying, this often pairs with a liquidity sweep — price tagging a premium-zone high, triggering breakout buyers and trapping their stop losses, right before institutional selling pressure takes over. A sweep occurring specifically inside the premium zone, rather than at a random point in the range, is treated as a more reliable signal of genuine distribution.

Premium & Discount with Market Structure

Premium and Discount becomes far more useful once it's layered on top of market structure rather than applied to an arbitrary swing. The most common approach is to define the range using the most recent significant structural leg — for example, the move from a confirmed swing low to the swing high that followed it, or vice versa — rather than picking an old high and low at random.

In a confirmed uptrend, traders typically look for buying opportunities specifically in the discount zone of the most recent bullish structural leg, treating premium as an area to take profit or avoid fresh longs rather than an area to sell against the trend. The reverse applies in a confirmed downtrend. The full method for identifying these structural legs is covered in the dedicated Market Structure guide.

Premium & Discount with Fair Value Gaps

A fair value gap sitting inside the discount zone of a bullish range carries more weight than the same gap sitting in premium, because it combines a statistically attractive price with the institutional footprint an FVG represents. Conversely, a bearish-context FVG found in the premium zone of a range is generally considered a stronger short opportunity than one found in discount.

This is one of the simplest and most common confluence checks experienced traders run before taking an FVG-based entry: locate the gap, then check which half of the relevant range it sits in. An FVG that lines up with the "right" side of equilibrium for the intended trade direction is treated as meaningfully stronger than one that doesn't. The mechanics of how these gaps form in the first place are covered in the dedicated FVG Guide.

Premium & Discount with Order Blocks

The same logic extends to order blocks. A bullish order block sitting in the discount zone of a range is generally treated as a higher-quality long opportunity than the identical-looking order block sitting in premium, simply because of where it falls relative to the broader range. A bearish order block in premium carries the same added weight for short setups.

Traders who skip this check sometimes end up taking technically valid order block setups at statistically poor prices — for example, a bullish order block sitting just below a range high, deep in premium, with very little room left before the range's upper extreme. Checking the order block's location against equilibrium first, before checking anything else about its validity, helps filter these lower-quality locations out early. The full criteria for validating an order block on its own terms are covered in the Order Blocks guide.

Trade Entry Examples

Premium and Discount works best as a filter layered on top of other concepts rather than a standalone trigger. Here's how that combination plays out in practice:

  1. Define the range. Use the most recent significant structural leg — a confirmed swing low to swing high, or the reverse — rather than an arbitrary high and low.
  2. Mark equilibrium. Find the 50% midpoint of that range, either manually or with a Fibonacci retracement tool set to the range's extremes.
  3. Wait for price to reach the appropriate half. For a long bias, wait for price to pull back into discount; for a short bias, wait for price to rally into premium.
  4. Layer in a point of interest. Look for an order block or fair value gap sitting inside that same half of the range, rather than treating the discount or premium zone alone as a precise entry trigger.
  5. Confirm with a lower timeframe signal. A CISD candle sequence or a small structural shift at the point of interest adds final confirmation before entry.
Real Example

Consider Bitcoin (BTC/USD) rallying from $58,000 to $64,000 on the 4-hour chart, then beginning to pull back. Equilibrium of that leg sits at $61,000. A bullish order block formed during the original rally sits at $59,800 — solidly inside the discount zone. A trader watching this would treat that order block as a higher-quality long opportunity specifically because of its discount-zone location, compared to an identical-looking order block that might have formed closer to $63,000, deep in premium, where the statistical case for buying is far weaker even if the candle pattern looks the same.

Key Takeaways

Premium and Discount splits a defined range at its 50% equilibrium point — above is premium (expensive), below is discount (cheap).

The concept doesn't predict direction on its own — it filters whether a buy or sell is happening at a statistically sensible price within the current range.

Institutions favor buying in discount and selling in premium because it leaves more room to average a large position favorably.

Order blocks and fair value gaps carry more weight when they fall on the statistically correct side of equilibrium for the intended trade direction.

Defining the range from a confirmed market structure leg, rather than an arbitrary high and low, makes Premium and Discount far more reliable.

Common Mistakes With Premium & Discount

1

Drawing the range from arbitrary highs and lows. Picking any visually obvious high and low instead of anchoring the range to a confirmed market structure leg produces an equilibrium line that doesn't reflect the actual trend context.

2

Treating Premium and Discount as a standalone signal. Buying simply because price is in discount, with no order block, FVG, or structural confirmation present, ignores that the concept is a filter, not an entry trigger on its own.

3

Ignoring which direction the broader trend favors. Looking for short setups in premium during a strong, confirmed uptrend fights the dominant order flow, even though the price is technically in the expensive half of a smaller range.

4

Confusing equilibrium with a guaranteed reaction zone. Expecting price to always bounce or reverse exactly at the 50% level overstates what equilibrium represents — it's a reference point for context, not a wall.

5

Re-using a stale range after it's been invalidated. Continuing to apply an old range's premium and discount zones after price has broken well beyond its high or low means the original equilibrium calculation no longer reflects current conditions.
